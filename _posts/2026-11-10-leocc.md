## Key Idea

On LEO networks like Starlink, the operator periodically reassigns which satellite serves a user, about every 15 seconds, and each reassignment changes bandwidth and RTT abruptly. Existing algorithms either underuse the link (Cubic, Copa, BBRv3) or build large queues (BBRv1, VIVACE), because they mix measurements from before and after the change. LeoCC detects these reconfigurations from the end host by pinging the ground station every 10 ms and flagging gaps between replies longer than about 45 ms. It then discards samples taken before the reconfiguration and briefly drains the queue before resuming. Against Cubic, Copa, and BBRv3 it achieves 85–494% higher throughput, and against BBRv1 and VIVACE 44–56% lower delay.

## Critique

The key insight is convincing: in LEO networks, abrupt path changes come from a schedule, not randomness, so a controller that knows when they happen can reset its model instead of treating them as congestion. The evaluation is also realistic, combining live Starlink terminals on three continents with a replay tool and over 4,800 public traces. But the design is tied to Starlink's current behavior: the 45 ms threshold is tuned empirically, the replay tool assumes Starlink queues ICMP separately, and an operator could change its schedule at any time. The per-device 10 ms probe also adds steady overhead, and the authors leave other constellations and harsher conditions to future work.
