## Key Idea

Hijack detectors such as ARTEMIS watch BGP announcements, so a careful attacker can evade them by steering its bogus route away from public monitors, for example with BGP communities. This paper looks at the data plane instead. If an attacker in another country intercepts traffic between two hosts in the same country and then forwards it on, the detour adds propagation delay that cannot be hidden: 1,000 km of extra fiber is about 5 ms of RTT. A geometric study of all 258 × 257 country pairs, using worst-case attacker and host placements, finds that 97% of cross-country interceptions add at least 5 ms at the speed of light, and 86–91% do once real RTT spreads from campus and M-Lab data are included. HiDe turns this into a detector on a Tofino2 switch at the network edge. It computes per-flow RTTs with Dart, keeps only the minimum RTT per /24 prefix in each 100 ms window, and flags an attack when that minimum jumps by more than λ ms and crosses the lowest RTT any route through the threat region could have. It then blocks the prefix and sends ICMP probes to unblock it if the surge was benign. On a 12-hour campus trace the false-positive rate is at most 0.012%, and a live PEERING hijack was caught within 0.5 s.

## Critique

The central argument is convincing. It does not try to stop all hijacks. It picks the subset that physics makes detectable and admits that short detours, such as within Europe or from the US through Canada, are out of reach. The weaknesses are in what "zero false negatives by design" quietly assumes. First, the detector only sees changes: an attack is flagged when minRTT jumps between two windows. An interception that is already in place during profiling is learned as normal, and HiDe then marks the prefix as one it cannot defend. Yet one of the paper's own motivating examples, China Telecom diverting US traffic for 2.5 years, is exactly that kind of attack. Second, the lower bound comes from IP geolocation of both endpoints. Many "US server prefixes" are anycast or CDN addresses, whose location databases are often wrong and whose RTTs can jump legitimately when the serving site changes. Third, mitigation means blocking the prefix, so every false positive is a self-inflicted outage, and an attacker who can briefly inflate RTTs could use HiDe to cut a network off from a service. The 0.012% rate is hard to judge without its denominator. If it is per prefix-window, thousands of prefixes with 100 ms windows could still produce a block every few seconds. Finally, the evidence is thin. The trace evaluation assumes the campus trace contains no attacks, and the live test is a single hijack (from Amsterdam) on a contrived Virginia–Ireland–campus path, where the absolute threshold sits only about 8.5 ms above the normal RTT.

## Connections

The background paper (Goldberg et al., SIGCOMM 2010) showed that even "secure" BGP variants leave many attacks open, especially when they are only partially deployed. Sixteen years later, with Rexford on both papers, this one stops waiting for deployment and defends a single network unilaterally. Like my [Biscay post](/2026/11/17/biscay/), it beats estimation with a signal from a layer the adversary or the protocol does not control: there the modem's grants, here the speed of light. It also extends my [partial reachability post](/2026/10/01/understanding-partial-reachability/): that paper showed that what the control plane says is reachable often differs from what the data plane delivers, and HiDe relies on the same gap in reverse, trusting what packets experience over what routes claim. Finally, the jurisdiction motive links to [VaulTor](/2026/11/19/vaultor/). Both papers treat where your traffic physically goes as a privacy property, which encryption alone does not give you.
