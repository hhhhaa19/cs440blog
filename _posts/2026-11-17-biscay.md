## Key Idea

On cellular paths the radio link is usually the bottleneck, and its bandwidth changes faster than end-to-end CCAs can track from RTT or loss. Biscay's view is that the phone does not need to estimate this bandwidth: the modem already knows how many bytes the base station granted it. OpenDiag, a kernel module, reads these grants from the Qualcomm diagnostic channel every ~10 ms instead of the ~1 s of MobileInsight or the Telephony API, by telling the modem to flush its debug buffer every millisecond. Biscay sets cwnd to the granted rate times RTT, splits that rate evenly across the device's active flows, and falls back to BBR when the end-to-end estimate shows a wired bottleneck. On 260 Pantheon traces it cuts average and tail delay by 58%/41% versus BBR and by ~99% versus CUBIC, with similar throughput.

## Critique

The best experiment in the paper is the sampling-interval sweep (Fig. 9). Delay only gets worse when KPIs arrive less often than every ~100 ms, and the coarse 100 ms "granted bytes" KPI performs as well as the per-TTI 3GPP formula. So the gain comes mostly from draining the modem buffer, not from 10 ms granularity or the in-kernel design. The paper does not discuss a basic issue: a grant reflects the base station's response to the phone's own buffer-status reports, so it measures what the phone asked for, not what the cell could give. A flow that sends less may receive smaller grants and never discover spare capacity. The trace-driven evaluation also favors Biscay, because its offline version replays a bandwidth trace derived from the same traces that define the emulated link, which makes it close to an oracle by construction. On a real Pixel 5, the gain over BBR shrinks to 4.6% more throughput and about 45% lower delay. That is still good, but far smaller than the ">90%" in the abstract. Finally, "unrooted" still means flashing a custom Android image and depending on an undocumented, Qualcomm-specific protocol, so deployment is up to OEMs, not users.
